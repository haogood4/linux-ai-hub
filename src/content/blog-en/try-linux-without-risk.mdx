# Three Risk-Free Ways to Try Linux, In Order

"Want to try Linux but afraid of breaking my computer" — the most common worry for beginners, and the one that needs the least worrying. The cost of trying Linux can be **close to zero**, as long as you pick the right method.

This post covers three ways, from lowest to highest risk. One suggestion: **go in this order, and only move on when you're happy with each step.**

## Way 1: Try in the browser (zero risk, 5 minutes)

Some distros offer online demos (e.g., the live experience mentioned in [Ubuntu's "try Ubuntu before you install"](https://ubuntu.com/tutorials/try-ubuntu-before-you-install), and online boot services like DistroSea). No downloads, no files touched — you can experience a real desktop in your browser.

- **Risk**: none. Nothing on your computer is touched
- **For**: people who just want to "see what Linux looks like"
- **Limits**: smoothness depends on your connection; it's experience-only, nothing is saved

## Way 2: Virtual machine (zero risk, recommended starting point)

Install a virtualization app on Windows/macOS (VirtualBox is free and open-source), then install Linux inside it. Linux runs inside a "virtual computer," fully isolated from your current system:

- **Risk**: none. Anything you do in the VM (including breaking it) never affects the host
- **For**: beginners who want to genuinely experience daily use and follow tutorials step by step
- **Limits**: limited graphics performance; not for gaming or heavy graphics

We have a full [virtual machine tutorial with screenshots](/try/virtualbox) — roughly 30 minutes to get running.

## Way 3: Boot from a USB stick (low risk, tests hardware compatibility)

Put Linux on a USB stick and boot your computer from it — nothing on the hard drive is touched. This is the most realistic "rehearsal" before installing to disk:

- **Risk**: low. Nothing is written to the hard drive, but the process of creating the bootable drive could select the wrong target disk — double-check while operating
- **For**: people who want to confirm their GPU, network card, and Wi-Fi work under Linux
- **Limits**: USB is slower than a hard drive, so the experience suffers; every boot starts from a clean state

Our [installation guide](/try/install) covers full Live USB creation and booting steps.

## When does "real installation" come in?

Once you've confirmed via VM / USB that daily operations are acceptable, common software has alternatives, and hardware all works — only then consider installing to disk (separate partition, dual boot, or full-disk install).

> **Important:** back up your important data before installing a system. Partitioning, formatting, or installing can cause data loss.

## Summary in one line

| Method | Risk | Time | What it validates |
|--------|------|------|-------------------|
| Browser demo | Zero | 5 min | What the desktop looks like |
| Virtual machine | Zero | 30 min | Daily use, following tutorials |
| USB boot | Low | 1 hour | Hardware compatibility |

Start risk-free, confirm each level, then install — by the end you'll have your own answer about whether Linux fits you, instead of someone else's.
